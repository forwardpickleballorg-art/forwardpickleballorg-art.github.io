--- title: "Why the Ball Pops Up in Pickleball" category: "Training" author: "Forward Pickleball" redirect_from: - /blog/2026/11/12/why-the-ball-pops-up/ ---

A ball that pops up at the kitchen line is a ball your opponent can put away. It happens to everyone — and it usually comes from one of four things, all of which start with your position.

1. Your paddle face is too open

This is the most direct cause. If your paddle face is pointing at the sky, the ball is going up. It often shows up as a scoop: the face opens under the ball and lifts it instead of pushing it forward. At the kitchen line, contact should be made with a fairly neutral paddle face.

2. You let the ball get behind you

When the ball gets behind your body, you have to lift it just to keep it out of the ground. That forced upward lift is what sends it up. Contact the ball out in front of you, and move your feet so you are behind every ball.

3. Your swing is too big

A big swing on a dink leaves a lot of room for error. You are much more likely to catch the ball late, or at an odd angle, and at the kitchen line there is not much distance between you and your opponent to hide that. Keep the swing compact on every dink.

4. Too much wrist

A flick of the wrist sends the ball up. The dink is a shoulder movement — a smooth push forward, almost like lifting the ball over the net — not a flick.

The thread that ties them together: your feet

These four are usually connected. When you do not move your feet and do not get behind the ball, you tend to hit it late — and when you hit it late, you tend to hit it with a more open paddle face. One late, flat-footed contact sets off the whole chain.

That is why footwork is often the real cause. It is easy to blame the swing, or the grip, or the paddle. But if you are in a better position — moved behind the ball, contact well out in front, ball kept from getting behind you — the chain does not start.

The takeaway: a popped-up ball is rarely one bad swing. It is usually a late contact, an open face, a big swing, or a wrist flick — and most often, feet that did not get you into position in the first place.
