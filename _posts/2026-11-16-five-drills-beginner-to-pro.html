--- title: "Five Drills That Take You From Beginner to Pro" category: "Training" author: "Forward Pickleball" redirect_from: - /blog/2026/11/16/five-drills-beginner-to-pro/ ---

Five drills that build on each other, from about a 3.0 level toward 5.0. Each one works on a different part of the game: footwork, contact point, power, feel, and fast hands. Work through them in order — each drill makes the next one easier.

1. Footwork: the glide shuffle

At the kitchen line, the goal is to move without rushing. The bread-and-butter footwork is a glide shuffle — the same basketball shuffle: little hop, slide, little hop back. One foot stays planted while the other hops, and you stay level, sliding across instead of bouncing up and down.

Stepping or lunging is fine when you need it. But the shuffle lets you line the ball up with your outside leg and get behind it in one simple motion, covering the most ground with the least effort. Go the wrong way and it takes extra steps, you end up off balance, and everything looks frantic. Slide, and you are always in position — and you can move slower, because you are never in a rush.

Level II: the butterfly drill. In the same half-court, one player hits dinks straight ahead while the partner hits dinks diagonally. Hit, recover to the middle, move, hit again. The key rule: recover to the middle between shots, or you will be out of position for the next ball. The target is 20 shots in a row — 10 each — and when you can do that consistently, you are ready for the next level.

2. Contact point: the same spot every time

On every shot — drive, drop, dink, reset — the ball should meet the paddle in the same place relative to your body: in front of your chest. Not too far out in front, not too far behind. That is how consistency starts.

Part 1: catching, from the baseline. Start at the baseline, because the ball comes faster there and a consistent contact point is harder. Catch the ball with your hand — no paddle. Catch it between your chest and hip, where you can make a complete swing. Too far out in front and you cannot turn through naturally; too far behind and you cannot get the elbow out. Everyone's exact spot is a little different — the point is finding yours and building the muscle memory of the timing. Wait, look the ball down, check it is in the right spot: that is a good rep.

Part 2: drop feeding. Drop the ball and hit it, lining up that same contact point. Start with the drive, because it is the fastest ball and the biggest swing — if you can hold the contact point there, the same motion applies to the drop, the dink, and the reset.

Part 3: with a partner, straight ahead. As your partner varies the distance, keep the contact point the same. On a shorter ball, wait a little longer; on a deeper ball, swing a little sooner. That is where most people get tripped up — they swing the same way when the ball changes location, instead of adjusting by that extra half second. If you lose the contact point in the partner drill, go back to part 1 or 2, re-dial it in, and come back.

3. Hitting hard the right way: the kinetic chain

A lot of players over-swing. Power does not come from swinging the arm faster — it comes from the ground up, through the kinetic chain: hips and core drive the shot, and the arm follows.

Feel it first: the partner palm-press. Face a partner, press your palms together, keep your wrists straight, and twist. You will feel it in your hips and core — that is how the shot should feel. Now test three hand positions: hands too close to the body and the lever is too small, nothing goes anywhere; hands too far back and the push is weak; hands out in front, lined up with the centre of your body, elbows straight, and you can push your partner back. That middle spot is the power position. (Too far in has its use too — that is a great position for a reset, when you do not want to apply force.)

Apply it: the torso twist, drop-fed. Drop the ball and let the hip twist bring the arm out. The only place you accelerate is the hip — you are not swinging fast with your arms. Keep the arms still and let the hips and core be the driver; keep the arm connected to the hip rotation and loose after contact. Push with your back hip through the shot: on a forehand for a right-handed player, the right hip pushes through; on the backhand, the left hip.

The extra 10%. Once the torso twist is comfortable, add a little hand acceleration: as you make contact, lift up with the shoulders and snap the wrist a little. That is the extra bit the pros get — but the bread and butter is the torso twist. The biggest killer of power is swinging too much with the arm and not letting the kinetic chain bring the paddle through.

4. Feel: the cornhole swing

Drops and soft shots need feel, and feel comes from a consistent swing. Start three steps back from the kitchen line and throw the ball underhand into the kitchen, like a cornhole toss — smooth, with a little extra hold at the end, leaning into it. That is the swing speed and tempo of a drop. Do it solo with a basket of balls, or with a partner doing the same from three steps back.

Keep that same smooth tempo when you hit the shot with the paddle. A lot of players switch from a smooth throw to a pokey, jabby, elbow-y swing the moment they pick up the paddle — that is how the feel disappears. Slow is smooth, smooth is fast.

Feel means being able to control the ball without swinging hard — placing it where you want it, with confidence, because you can hold the ball on the paddle longer. The faster you swing, the less precise the placement gets.

Make it competitive. Put a target down — a paddle laid on the court — and try to hit it. Or put 10 push-ups on it with a partner. A little pressure in the drill makes it feel more like a match, and that gets you ready.

5. Fast hands: the wall drill

Start on a wall, volleying back and forth: one forehand, one backhand, one hand or two on the paddle. Two things this builds: a better ready position and finish, and control of your backswing tempo. Keep a nice rhythm — the finish of one shot naturally slides into the next spot. If it looks out of control, slow it down.

Fast hands is really about anticipation. Your paddle follows the ball: if the ball goes one way, your paddle goes that way too. The goal is 20 in a row at a good tempo, with the ball coming to about chest level. If the ball is bouncing up and down and all over the place, the tempo needs work. Keep the swings small — small pushing motions, not fast swings — and keep the wrist locked. If you are struggling, slow it down and let the ball drop onto the paddle, then push through.

Into a point. Now take that compactness into a point scenario. Watch out for too big a backswing, swinging too fast, or getting too wristy — in a point your blood pressure rises and you get antsy, so this is a good way to calm it down.

Advanced: the three-shot drill. Feed the ball, your partner dinks, you speed up, they reset; you dink, they speed up, you reset. Keep going: dink, speed up, reset. Same compact swings, just pushing through — a big backswing and it all falls apart.

Elastic band trick. Put an elastic band around your arms, over your head, to force compactness — if your elbow naturally drifts out or gets behind you, the band keeps the shots small and simple. Use it with care: if you lift too much it can snap up.

The takeaway: five parts, in order — footwork that keeps you in position without rushing, a contact point that never moves, power from the hips and core instead of the arm, a smooth swing that gives you feel, and compact fast hands. Put them together and they cover the game.
