Pickleball · Shot Technique

Forehand Drops That Earn You to the Net

Written by ForwardPickleball.org · https://forwardpickleball.org

If your forehand drop feels like a coin flip, the problem usually isn't nerves — it's that you're using one technique for jobs that need three different shots. The lift, the shovel, and the topspin drop each solve a different problem, and knowing which one to reach for can transform your transition game.

The forehand drop is the shot that earns you the trip from the baseline to the net — and it's not one shot, it's three. Strong players choose their drop based on where the ball is, how much time they have, and what shot came before it. That single decision, made correctly, can dramatically raise your drop percentage without changing a thing about your swing.

FOUNDATION

Balance and Grip Come Before Everything

Applies to: all three drops, every time.

Before choosing a technique, build the platform. Every drop starts from a wide, low stance with your weight loaded into your dominant foot — open stance or a slight step in, it doesn't matter, as long as you're stable and your arm has a steady base to work from. If the base is off, no amount of arm skill will rescue the shot.

Then check your hand. Grip pressure should sit around a four out of ten — light enough to feel the ball, firm enough to control the face. Squeezing harder injects tension that kills touch. And through contact on every variation, the wrist stays locked and passive: an active, flipping wrist is the single biggest consistency killer in the drop game.

The non-negotiables

Wide stance, weight into the dominant foot, body low. Grip pressure ~4/10. Wrist quiet through contact — the shoulder does the work on every drop you'll ever hit.

DROP 01

The Lift Drop — Your Everyday Workhorse

Use it when: the ball is low and clearly in front of you, and you have time.

The lift drop (also called the push drop) should be the shot you hit most often. It has the fewest moving parts of the three, which is exactly why it holds up under pressure.

The defining mechanic is the laid-back wrist: hinge your wrist back so the paddle face stays open and pointed at the ball. That laid-back position lets you make contact well out in front of your body — a stronger hitting position that lets you accelerate forward through the ball without any flip at contact. From there, the shoulder drives a smooth low-to-high motion while the wrist just goes along for the ride.

Key point: lead with your hand, not the top of the paddle, and keep your feet quiet at contact. Boring, repeatable, effective — that's the whole job of the lift drop.

DROP 02

The Shovel Drop — Your Escape Route

Use it when: the ball gets deep into your stance, at your feet, or behind you.

Sometimes the lift drop simply isn't available. You drove your third shot, your opponent blocked a fast, deep reply at your feet, and now the ball is past the contact zone where a laid-back wrist can reach it. That's the shovel drop's moment.

The difference is entirely in the wrist. Where the lift drop hinges the wrist back, the shovel hinges it the opposite way — opening the paddle face so you can scoop up from underneath the ball at your side, near the back leg, or even slightly behind you. The wrist still stays stable through contact; your shoulder and elbow drive the scoop. Only the contact zone and the direction of the hinge change.

One honest warning from the lesson: this is not a beginner shot. Hold off on the shovel until you're playing around the 4.0 level or above — below that, its mechanics tend to create more inconsistency than they solve. Build the lift drop first.

Key point: bend from the knees, not the back, to get down to these balls. The shovel lives near your feet, and your legs are what make its low contact point sustainable.

DROP 03

The Topspin Drop — Pressure, Earned

Use it when: it's a third shot, the return was weak, and you have time to fully set up.

The topspin drop is the most aggressive of the three — and the most misunderstood. The spin does not come from snapping the wrist. It comes from a steep, vertical swing path driven by a bicep contraction and shoulder motion: the paddle tip drops below the ball, then brushes up the back of it while the wrist stays relatively stable. If you're pushing forward instead of swinging upward, you're not hitting a topspin drop; you're just hitting a faster flat one.

Because it's demanding, it needs the right conditions: time to get fully behind the ball with your feet, room to drop the paddle tip before contact, space to load into your dominant leg to help the upward drive, and a return soft enough to let all of that happen. That's why it belongs on third shots after a weak return — not on fifth shots when you're already moving and pressed for time.

Key point: do not rush the net after a topspin drop. It's a deep, fast ball — stay back, wait for the weak reply it creates, and then use a lift or shovel drop on the next ball to make your move in. Charging in right after is one of the most common positioning mistakes at the 4.0–4.5 level.

DECISION

Which Drop, When?

The fast read: where is the ball, and how much time do you have?

The lift drop is your default whenever the ball is low and clearly in front of you with time to spare — the classic case is your opponent volleying your drive back short and soft. The shovel takes over the moment the ball travels deeper than your lift contact zone, usually because an aggressive drive of yours came back fast and pinned you. And the topspin drop is a third-shot special: a weak return, a full setup, and the chance to plant a dipping ball at their feet that buys you the weak reply — and the trip to the net — two shots later.

FIX THESE

The Three Mistakes That Sink Most Drops

First, the active wrist. On all three variations, the wrist must be locked and stable at the moment of contact — every extra wrist movement adds a variable, and variables destroy consistency. Second, the wrong tool for the job: forcing a lift drop on a ball that's already behind you produces an awkward, doomed contact point that the shovel exists precisely to fix. Third, lazy feet. Your footwork sets up your contact zone, and if you arrive late or off balance, no amount of arm mechanics will save the shot.

Practice order: master the lift drop first — it's the foundation everything else stands on. Add the shovel as your game reaches 4.0, and bring in the topspin drop from midcourt before you ever trust it from the baseline.

GOOD TO KNOW

How a Drop Is Different From a Reset

Two soft shots, two different jobs — and the reset is won or lost from the legs down.

Drops and resets look similar from the sideline: both are soft, both land in the kitchen, both use a light grip and a quiet wrist. But they answer opposite questions. A drop is a shot you choose. You have time, the ball has bounced, and you're deliberately hitting an unattackable ball to earn your team's trip to the net. It's construction — the lift, shovel, and topspin variations above are all tools for building your way forward.

A reset is a shot that chooses you. You're caught in the transition zone or at the line, your opponent is attacking, and the ball is coming fast at your body or feet — often before it ever bounces. There's no swing to speak of: you absorb the pace with soft hands and a near-motionless block, cushioning the ball so it dies in the kitchen and the attack ends. The goal isn't to advance; it's to survive the exchange and drag the rally back to neutral, so that a ball you can drop shows up next.

The legs do the resetting

Here's the part most players miss: while the drop is a shoulder shot, the reset is a leg shot. The hands only look soft because the legs underneath them have already done four jobs.

First, split-step and stop. The instant your opponent commits to the attack, land in a small split-step — feet a little wider than shoulders, weight on the balls of both feet — and kill your forward momentum completely. Trying to reset while still running through the transition zone is nearly impossible; a moving body turns a soft block into an accidental volley that pops up.

Second, sink from the knees — never the waist. Attacks come in low, aimed at your feet, so your paddle has to get level with or under the ball. Bending at the waist drops your head and eyes, wrecks your balance, and leaves the paddle high. Bending the knees drops your entire center of gravity as one unit, keeps your chest up and eyes on the ball, and brings the paddle down into the hitting zone naturally.

Third, stay planted through contact. With your base wide and low, your legs act like shock absorbers: knees flexed, weight centred, nothing lunging or drifting as the ball arrives. The stillness in your legs is precisely what lets your hands stay soft — a stable base absorbs pace, a moving one feeds it back.

Fourth, push off and creep forward. Once your reset dies in the kitchen, the legs go back to work: push off the balls of your feet, take two or three small controlled steps forward — never a sprint — and be ready to split-step again the moment the next attack comes. That's how you claw through the transition zone: reset, creep, split, reset, until you've earned the line.

Quick test in the moment: did the ball bounce and give you time? Drop. Is it fast, at your body, before the bounce? Stop your feet, sink, and reset. If your legs are still moving at contact, the pop-up isn't your hands' fault — it's your feet's.

Three drops, three jobs. The lift gets you through the everyday rallies, the shovel bails you out when the ball beats your feet, and the topspin turns a weak return into real pressure. Learn to pick the right one before the ball arrives, and the shot that used to feel like a coin flip becomes the most dependable part of your transition game.

The One-Line Takeaway

The forehand drop isn't one shot — it's three. Quiet wrist, low base, light grip on all of them; then let the ball's position pick the technique: lift when it's in front, shovel when it's behind, topspin when a weak return gives you time to make them pay.
