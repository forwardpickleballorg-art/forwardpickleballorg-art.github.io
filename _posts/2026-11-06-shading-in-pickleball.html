If you and your partner keep losing points down the middle, the problem usually isn’t your hands. It’s where you’re standing when the ball is struck.

Shading fixes that. You and your partner slide together toward the side of the court where the ball is, so the angles your opponents can hit shrink before they even swing.

Shading works hand in hand with tethering. Tethering is the invisible rope, about 10 feet long, between you and your partner: when one moves, the other moves. Shading is the direction that rope slides: toward the ball.

Why it’s called “shading”

The word is borrowed from other sports. A baseball infielder “shades” a step or two toward where a hitter usually hits. A football safety shades toward the strong side of the formation. In both, it means a slight lean toward one side, not a full move.

That’s exactly what happens on a pickleball court: a small, deliberate tilt of your team’s position toward the ball.

It’s an analogy from art. When you shade a drawing, you add more pencil to one area to make it darker, and leave other areas lighter. You aren’t redrawing the picture, just putting more weight where it matters.

On court it’s the same idea. Your team “darkens” the part of the court where the ball can go by putting more coverage there, and leaves the far side “lighter” — a bit more open — because a shot there is a long crosscourt ball that gives you time. Shading, step by step

The whole skill fits in one rule: where the ball goes, your team goes. Here’s how to do it on court.

  1. Get tethered at the kitchen line. Stand about 10 feet apart, each roughly centred on your half, paddles up. Before the game, agree who owns the middle. A good rule is that whoever can reach the ball earliest takes it, not automatically the forehand. Something as simple as “I’ve got middle unless you call me off” settles it.
  2. Read the ball, not the bodies. Your cue is where the ball is on the other side of the net. Ball in the middle: stay centred. Ball pulled toward one sideline: time to shade.
  3. Shift together, two or three steps. Both of you slide toward the ball’s side with small side shuffles. Two or three steps is plenty. The rope between you stays the same length, so no gap opens.
  4. Ball-side player guards the line. The player directly across from the ball protects the sideline. Don’t drift toward the middle and leave the down-the-line shot open.
  5. Off-ball partner closes the middle. The other player slides toward the centre line, a touch behind their partner, ready for the counter. The middle is the most dangerous gap. The far sideline is a long crosscourt ball, so there’s more time to get there.
  6. Be set before they hit. Finish the shift and split-step as your opponent makes contact. If you’re still moving at contact, a ball at your feet or just past your paddle wins the point.
  7. Reset as the ball moves. Ball back to the middle: return to centre. Ball to the other side: mirror the shift. Every new shot is a new read.

Two adjustments sit on top of these steps. When you’re under attack, tighten up to roughly 6 to 8 feet apart so you can cover fast exchanges. When your attack pushes the other team back, move in together and take their time away.

Talk while you move

Shading breaks down when partners guess. One-word calls keep you moving as a pair.

CallWhat it tells your partner
“Shift”The ball moved, slide with me
“Middle” or “Mine”I’m taking the ball down the centre
“Yours”Your ball, I’m covering behind you
“Squeeze”We’re on the attack, step in together
“Switch”We’re trading sides, take my spot

Common slips and quick fixes

Most shading errors come from one player moving and the other standing still.

SlipWhat happensFix
Guarding “my half”The middle opens upGuard the ball’s angles, not a side
Partner pulled wide, you stay putA big hole down the centreSlide inside the moment they move
Over-shading, sprinting to the sidelineThe middle and crosscourt open behind youTwo or three steps, then stop
Still moving at contactCaught off balance, ball at your feetGet set and split-step before they hit
Moving up as a ladder, one up and one backA gap between you and the netMove forward side by side, same depth
Forgetting to resetStuck on one side when the ball returnsFollow the ball back to centre

Drills to make it automatic

Shading feels awkward until it’s automatic, and casual games rarely get it there. A few focused minutes before open play will.

  1. Rope walk (5 minutes). You and your partner hold either end of a 10-foot rope or long resistance band at the kitchen line. A third player points left, centre or right. Shuffle two or three steps together without letting the rope go slack or tight.
  2. Feeder reads (10 minutes). A feeder moves around the other side and dinks from different spots. Before each feed, shade to match the feeder’s position, then play the ball. The feeder can call “freeze” at contact so you can check where you’re both standing.
  3. Pull-wide dink game (10 minutes). Play 2-on-2 dinks where the other team aims for sharp angles. Any ball that gets through the middle counts double. It quickly exposes the partner who doesn’t slide in.
  4. Freeze-frame video check. Film a game from behind the baseline. Pause at each opponent contact and ask two questions: did both of us shift, and is the gap between us the same?

The takeaway

Shading won’t change a single stroke, and that’s the point. Two players with average hands who move as one team will beat two better hitters playing their own halves. Stay tethered, follow the ball, and let your partner know where you’re going.
