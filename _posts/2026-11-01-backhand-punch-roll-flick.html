Most backhand volleys feel inconsistent for a simple reason: players use one motion for three different shots. The punch, the roll, and the flick each need a different setup, contact point, and swing path. Learn them separately, and you can choose the right one in a rally instead of hoping one motion covers everything.

Pick by ball height first

  • Below net height: punch — your safest, slowest option. Keep it low.
  • At net height, or a little below: roll — topspin and control, your middle ground.
  • Above net height: flick — your fastest, most powerful option.

Pace helps disguise the choice, too. As a guide, the punch is the slowest, around 30–40 mph, the roll is a little faster at 40–50 mph with the most topspin, and the flick is fastest at 50–60+ mph. If all three come at the same pace, opponents can read your setup and prepare the same reply every time.

1. The Punch: safe, low, and hard to punish

Use the punch when the ball sits below net height and you want a low, predictable attack. It may not win the point outright, but it catches your opponent off balance and sets up your next shot.

Setup: get close to the kitchen line so you can reach in with control. Drop your paddle tip down toward the floor so you get underneath the ball.

How it moves: keep your elbow locked — it does not unlock or swing through in any direction. Your wrist and shoulder do the work together — a short wrist snap, with your shoulder shoving through the ball at the same time. Snap through the ball, not up it.

Watch out for: opening your paddle face toward the sky. That sends the ball high and hands your opponent an easy put-away. Because the motion is compact and steady, you can attack from well below the net without popping the ball up.

2. The Roll: topspin and control

The roll is the middle ground that works in most situations: more topspin than the punch, more control than the flick.

Setup: this is where the roll differs from the punch. Drop the paddle corner below the ball, not the whole paddle tip, and drop your whole hand below the ball as well. Sink into your legs to get that corner down low. Dropping the whole tip invites too much wrist, and the roll is a shoulder-led shot, not a wrist-led one.

Contact: look for a little elbow extension as you meet the ball. A bent elbow gets you jammed and pops the ball up with little power. Overreaching is just as costly — if you break your body or arm position to reach it, or your weight shifts onto your toes, you are reaching too far. Stay athletic, hips fairly still, weight centred on your midfoot.

How it moves: your shoulder lifts the paddle from knee height up toward shoulder height. Your wrist only opens the face slightly through contact. Think shoulder motion about double the wrist motion. If the shot feels wristy, the balance is off.

Swing path: extend through the ball a little, then lift with your shoulder. Setting up too close and swinging straight up — an “elevator” swing — gives the ball no action and leads to net errors.

A drill that helps: put light pressure on your wrist, almost like a cast, so it can barely move. That forces your shoulder to get under and up the ball. After a few rounds like that, add a little wrist back in — you will have trained the right ratio. The extension-versus-lift balance is personal, so drill it until you find yours.

Do-not-attack check: if you feel overextended — hips back, shoulder lunging, weight on your toes — do not force an attacking volley from there. Reset and get set again.

3. The Flick: maximum power

Use the flick when the ball is above net height and you want to attack with real pace. Below net height, there is not enough room to generate the power that makes this shot worth it. It has more moving parts than the other two, so it takes more training to master.

Setup: get as close to the kitchen line as you can without foot-faulting, with your chest engaged over the line. You are about to generate serious power, so set up for it.

How it moves: this is the one backhand volley where your elbow joins in. From a normal handshake grip, hinge your wrist so the paddle tip points off to the side — that stores energy. Point your elbow toward your target to store more. Then snap your wrist and elbow together, with rotation from your shoulder and midback. Bring your shoulder around with your elbow when you have time.

Why rotation matters: wrist and elbow alone give you a certain amount of power. Add shoulder and midback rotation and you can roughly double it. A higher ball gives you more time to rotate fully; on a quicker, lower ball, simplify to wrist and elbow. Drive mainly through the ball with extension, since it is above net height — a little topspin is normal, but pace is the main goal here, not disguise by a late change of direction. The flick also needs the most training of the three, because it has the most moving parts.

Where to attack with them

When players protect their body, most set up on their backhand side. That makes an early down-the-line roll a smart attack: it forces a difficult choice. Your opponent either slides into a chicken-wing position, which is hard to execute and makes their counter predictable, or opens up on a forehand and has to punch through the ball — a low-percentage counter if they get wristy from there.

Attacking down the line also keeps the next ball predictable for you. You stay backhand to backhand, so you can get set again quickly for the reply, instead of chasing a ball across your body. After that roll, open your paddle toward that line and lean toward it, expecting the next ball back in the same general area — an inside attack, by contrast, is more likely to bring the next ball back to your forehand.

Test these cues in live-ball drills, too. The breakdowns behind them were shown mainly on a ball stand with slow-motion detail, not in live rally exchanges, so live practice is where you confirm what works for you.

Put it into your game

Drill each shot on its own before you mix them in live play. Start with the setup — footwork, paddle position, and hand placement decide everything that follows. Then train yourself to read ball height first, and only then choose punch, roll, or flick.
