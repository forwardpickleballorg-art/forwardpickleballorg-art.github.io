There is some truth to the idea that players do not use their elbow in pickleball, but it is better put a little differently: players often do not understand how to use the elbow as part of an efficient paddle movement.

The elbow matters because it helps control the paddle, keeps shots consistent, and generates power without relying entirely on the wrist or shoulder.

Why players struggle with elbow mechanics

Nobody told them the elbow is a hinge

Their mental model is "hit the ball with the paddle," so the whole arm moves as one piece. The idea that the arm is two levers joined at the elbow just is not in their picture yet — you cannot use a tool you do not know exists.

Arm too straight

Players often lock their elbow, making paddle movement stiff and difficult to control. They think power comes from the arm, so they wind up a big swing — and a big swing with a locked elbow feels powerful, once. But the elbow never folds, so there is no hinge, no lever, and no repeatability. The power they feel is the shoulder muscling through, not the core driving a lever.

Too much wrist movement

When the elbow does not hinge, something else has to create the paddle movement, and it is usually the wrist. Players try to generate power by flicking the wrist instead of coordinating the arm and paddle — and that is why their balls pop up or spray. The wrist is doing the elbow's job, badly.

Elbow too far from the body

When the elbow drifts away from the body, shots get less compact and control drops, especially during kitchen-line exchanges. The paddle ends up far from the body, the swing gets long, and the next shot is harder to prepare for. It is an issue because the elbow's distance from the body decides how long and how strong the lever is — and at the kitchen, long and weak loses points.

The swing gets long and slow. Elbow out means the paddle travels a bigger arc to reach the ball and a bigger arc to come back. At the kitchen, balls arrive in a fraction of a second — a long swing is still on its way when the ball is already past you. Compact wins hands battles; long loses them.

You lose the body's support. When the elbow is tucked near the body, the arm is connected to the core — the core and shoulder do the work and the arm just carries it. Elbow away from the body disconnects the arm from the core, so the shoulder and arm muscles have to do everything alone. Weaker, less consistent, and tiring over a long game.

Small errors get bigger. A long lever amplifies mistakes. If your shoulder rotation is off by a little, the paddle face at the end of an extended arm is off by a lot. With the elbow in, the same small error barely moves the paddle face — that is why compact shots stay consistent and extended ones spray.

You cannot adjust mid-swing. At the kitchen, balls change direction fast — a speed-up at your body, a ball that dips. With the elbow out and the swing already long, there is no room to change direction or shorten the swing. You get handcuffed on balls at the body, because the paddle is already committed out wide.

It pulls you off balance. Reaching with the elbow out shifts your weight toward the ball and away from centre. You are off balance for the next ball, and the recovery back to ready takes longer — in a fast exchange, that late recovery is the point.

The shoulder pays for it. With the elbow extended away from the body, the shoulder carries the whole load of the arm and paddle. That is a small joint doing a big job, and it is how shoulders get sore and elbows get strained.

They are late, so they brace

Players read the ball late. When the ball is already on them, the body stiffens and the elbow locks — it is a bracing reflex, not a swing. The hinge only works when there is time to set it: elbow in front, slightly bent, before the swing starts. Late means reaching, and reaching means a straight arm.

Grip too tight

A death grip locks the wrist and elbow together, so the whole arm becomes one rigid stick. A hinge cannot hinge when everything around it is clenched.

No shoulder turn, so nothing to hinge against

The elbow hinge works because the shoulder turn and core rotation drive it. Players often stand square and swing the arm alone, so the elbow never gets loaded in the first place — there is no turn for it to unload from.

They cannot feel it yet

The hinge is a small, subtle movement, and players are watching the ball, not their body. They do not have the feel for what a loaded elbow versus a locked elbow even is, and they do not maintain a comfortable elbow bend from shot to shot, which makes it harder to prepare for the next one.

Elbow position by situation

The elbow does not sit the same way for every shot. Here is the principle for each situation:

SituationElbow principle
DinkingRelaxed, slightly bent, compact movement
VolleysStable elbow, compact paddle movement
Forehand driveAllow elbow to bend and extend naturally
BackhandMaintain comfortable spacing and avoid locking the arm
ResetRelaxed elbow, soft hands, controlled paddle face

Do not force the elbow

One important distinction: we do not want players to consciously move their elbow on every shot. The elbow should work naturally as part of the kinetic chain — the shoulder turn and core rotation drive it, and the elbow carries that power to the paddle. Think of it as structure, not a move to perform.

What to teach

Three things:

  • Relax the shoulder.
  • Keep a comfortable elbow bend.
  • Let the paddle move efficiently rather than forcing the shot.

Practise short swings — dinks are perfect for it — until the forearm swinging on the hinge starts to feel normal. Once they feel it once, they start using it everywhere.

What it looks like on court

Big backswings, arm-only shots, wrist flicks, balls into the net or sailing long, and sore shoulders and elbows — because the joints are absorbing force the core should be handling.

The takeaway: it is not about using more elbow; it is about using the elbow efficiently. For players developing from 2.5 to 3.5, learning compact and efficient mechanics can make a significant difference.
