--- title: "Why Beginners Lock Their Arm Straight" category: "Beginners" author: "Forward Pickleball" redirect_from: - /blog/2026/11/26/why-beginners-lock-their-arm-straight/ ---

Yesterday I visited a beginner's court with 26 players. Most of them have been playing pickleball for over a year, but they are not fully engaged with proper body mechanics. The most common thing I saw: arms locked straight.

Beginners almost always lock their arm straight because of survival instinct, poor spacing, and cross-sport habits. When a ball comes at them, the brain tries to create maximum distance between the body and the incoming object while maximizing reach.

The four root causes

1. Reaching instead of moving (poor footwork)

Beginners stand still and let the paddle do the traveling. Locking the elbow buys an extra 8–12 inches of reach when they fail to adjust their feet to the ball's bounce.

2. The ping-pong / tennis cross-over

Players coming from table tennis tend to swing from the elbow with a straight lever, while beginner tennis players often lock their elbow trying to mimic a straight-arm forehand — without having the proper shoulder rotation or core engagement to support it.

3. Arming the ball (lack of kinetic chain)

Without understanding that power comes from the legs, hips, and shoulder rotation, beginners try to generate pace by swinging their entire arm like a rigid pendulum or baseball bat.

4. Fear of missing / jamming

A straight arm feels predictable — it creates a single, simple hinge at the shoulder. When beginners bend their elbow, they introduce a second moving joint that they do not yet know how to stabilize, causing them to mistime the ball or hit the edge of the paddle.

Why it hurts their game

A locked elbow ruins touch, reset ability, and reaction speed at the kitchen line. When the arm is fully extended, there is zero shock absorption — hard drives bounce uncontrollably off the paddle face instead of dropping into the kitchen, and the paddle tip drops, making quick volleys nearly impossible.

Two coaching fixes

To break the habit, focus on two immediate cues:

  • Keep the elbow in front of the torso with a soft bend (roughly 100°–120°), acting as a shock absorber rather than a rigid stick.
  • Move the feet to keep the contact point in the “hitting zone” (about a foot in front of the lead hip) so the arm does not have to reach out to rescue a late setup.
