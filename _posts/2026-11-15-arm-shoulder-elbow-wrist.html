--- title: "Shoulder, Elbow, Wrist: How Your Arm Strikes the Ball" category: "Training" author: "Forward Pickleball" redirect_from: - /blog/2026/11/15/arm-shoulder-elbow-wrist/ ---

Start with relaxed shoulders square to your target. Keep your elbow slightly flexed and in front of your body to act like a hinge, and lock your wrist in a firm, slightly laid-back position for control. Finally, power your swing from your core and shoulder, keeping that arm structure steady throughout the motion.

Shoulders

Relaxed, down, not shrugged up toward your ears. Square to your target at contact — if your shoulders are already open, the swing has nowhere to go. The shoulder turn is what loads the shot: turn the shoulders back as the ball comes, then let them rotate through. Tension in the shoulders kills that rotation, so relaxed is doing real work here.

Elbow — the hinge and the leverage

Yes, there is leverage, and the elbow is the hinge of it. Think of your arm as two levers joined at the elbow: upper arm and forearm-plus-paddle. The shoulder and core supply the force; the elbow holds the angle so that force reaches the paddle instead of leaking away.

Slightly flexed, in front of your body. That bent elbow keeps the paddle in front of you, where contact should happen. If the elbow drifts behind your body, the paddle is late and the arm gets stuck — you end up arming the ball or flicking the wrist to catch up.

A bent elbow is a short, strong lever. Short lever means control and quickness — that is why dinks and volleys at the kitchen work with the elbow tucked in front and barely moving. Straighten the arm and the lever gets longer: more reach and more pace on a drive, but slower and harder to control. So the elbow angle is your dial between control and power.

The hinge action. On the backswing the elbow folds a little, loading the forearm; through the swing it holds steady or extends slightly, letting the forearm swing through like a hinge swinging a door. The elbow itself does not power the shot — it stays stable while the core and shoulder rotate. A locked-straight elbow has no hinge at all, so the whole arm swings stiff and the shoulder does everything, which is how you lose both pace and accuracy, and how elbows get sore.

Wrist

Firm and slightly laid back, locked through the whole motion. A firm wrist keeps the paddle face steady at contact — the moment the wrist breaks or flips, the face opens and the ball pops up. The laid-back position also gives a little lag: the paddle trails slightly behind the hand and then comes through square. No flicking, except on the shots that are built around it, like the flick itself.

How the strike goes, in order

  1. Set the structure: shoulders relaxed and square, elbow slightly bent and in front, wrist firm and slightly laid back.
  2. Shoulder turn loads the shot as the ball comes.
  3. Step in, weight transfers forward, core starts rotating.
  4. Shoulder drives the arm through — the elbow holds its angle, the forearm swings on the hinge.
  5. Contact in front of your body, paddle face square, wrist still firm.
  6. Follow through — short at the kitchen, fuller on a drive — then back to ready.

What goes wrong

Elbow behind the body means late contact and arm-only shots. Elbow locked straight means a stiff, slow swing. Wrist breaking at contact means pop-ups. Shoulders tense and shrugged means the core cannot rotate, so the arm tries to do all the work.

The takeaway: the core and shoulder are the engine, the elbow is the hinge that carries that power to the paddle, and the wrist is the lock that keeps it from leaking.
