Fast hands: winning the kitchen volley battle

When both teams reach the kitchen line, points are decided in fractions of a second. Here's how to build the compact, calm volleys that win firefights.

Why fast hands win points

Above 4.0, the kitchen line is where points are decided.

Once both teams are established at the kitchen line, dinks only last so long — someone speeds the ball up, and the firefight begins. The team with quicker, more compact hands wins these exchanges far more often than the team swinging harder.

Here's the counterintuitive truth: fast hands come from doing less, not more. Shorter swings, a quieter body, and a calmer mind beat frantic hacking every time.

Keys to lightning volleys

Five fundamentals for the hands battle.

Paddle up and in front

Keep your paddle at chest height, out in front of your body, before the ball is even hit. If your paddle drops to your waist between shots, you're already late — every reset of the paddle costs you a tenth of a second you don't have.

Kill the backswing

Block and punch — don't swing. The power is already in the incoming ball; your job is to redirect it. Think catch, not hit. A compact 6-inch punch beats a full swing that arrives late.

Soft hands, firm wrist

Grip at 4–5 out of 10 with the wrist locked. Soft fingers absorb the ball's pace so it doesn't fly; a firm wrist keeps the paddle face stable so it doesn't wobble. Loose grip plus floppy wrist is how balls end up in the net — or the fence.

Read the paddle, not the ball

By the time you react to the ball, it's too late. Watch your opponent's paddle face at contact — its angle tells you where the ball is going before it leaves the strings. This one habit buys you the extra split-second that makes volleys feel slow.

Stay low and loaded

Athletic stance, knees bent, weight on the balls of your feet, leaning slightly forward. You can't explode in any direction standing tall — and in a firefight, the first step is everything.

The firefight drill

The fastest way to build hands that don't panic.

How to run it

  1. Set up: Both players at the kitchen line, paddles up. Start with a cooperative dink.
  2. Ignite: Either player speeds up any ball — from there it's a live rapid-fire volley exchange. No stepping back behind the kitchen line.
  3. Rules: Keep every swing compact. First to miss loses the rally. Play first to 7, then switch the player who initiates.
  4. Progress it: Start at 70% pace until rallies last 6+ shots, then build to full speed. Add a second ball: when one rally ends, the feeder immediately tosses the next — no time to overthink.

The goal isn't to win every exchange — it's to stay compact and calm under fire. Players who try to crush every volley tighten up and miss; players who stay small and redirect win the long rallies.

Counter-attacking the speed-up

Don't just survive the attack — punish it.

Every speed-up is a risk for the attacker too. A well-executed counter turns their aggression into your winner — and the secret is simple: let their pace do the work.

Absorb and redirect

When the ball is coming in hot, don't swing — angle your paddle face and let the pace rebound it back. Aim the redirect at open court or at the attacker's partner, who isn't expecting it.

Punch through when you're set

If you read the speed-up early and your feet are set, drive a short punch through the ball — right at the attacker's feet or backhand hip. Set feet means green light; moving feet means just get it back.

Target the feet

Counters aimed at the feet are the hardest to handle. The attacker is usually still drifting forward after their speed-up — a ball at their shoelaces forces a weak reply or a pop-up you can put away.

One look, three shots

Keep the exact same compact prep whether you're dinking, blocking, or countering. If your wind-up changes, good opponents read it and back off. Disguise is what turns a good counter into an untouched winner.

The counter-attack drill

  1. Set up: Both players at the kitchen line. Player A is the attacker, Player B the counterer.
  2. Pattern: A dinks twice, then speeds up any ball. B must counter-attack — not just block — aiming at A's feet or the middle.
  3. Scoring: B scores for every counter that forces a miss or a weak reply; A scores for every speed-up B can't handle. First to 7.
  4. Progress it: The attacker must disguise the speed-up with no tell; the counterer must hold the same ready position on every ball. Then switch roles.
Calm hands are fast hands

Tension is the enemy of speed. Relax your grip, shorten your swing, quiet your feet — and watch the game slow down.
